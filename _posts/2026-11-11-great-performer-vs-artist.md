AI is changing the way we work at a frightening pace. And recently, I've been struggling with a bit of a dilemma.

AI can already do many things better than I can. So naturally, I've been trying to figure out what I can do better than AI. What is left for me to contribute?

But the more I think about it, the less convincing this approach feels.

As Logan pointed out to me, we've seen this pattern so many times. People say AI cannot do something, often with pretty good reasons. Then technology improves, and suddenly it turns out AI can do it. The boundary keeps moving.

So maybe trying to find something AI cannot do is not a particularly good long-term strategy.

What should we do instead? How should we approach this technology, especially as researchers?

For the sake of discussion, let's make a strong assumption. Imagine an AI that has overcome the limitations we often talk about today: poor physical understanding, reward hacking, unreliable reasoning, and so on. Assume it can actually understand physics, design meaningful experiments, recognize its own mistakes, and carry out scientific research end to end.

In other words, let's not make the argument depend on what AI currently cannot do.

If AI can do science as well as, or even better than, we can, what does it mean for us to be scientists?

I've been thinking about this question quite a lot. And strangely, one persuasive answer came from Jazz. I like playing jazz piano and listening to jazz.

In jazz, there is a part called a solo, where musicians improvise over the chord progression of a song. There are some basic rules, but a lot of freedom to show creativity and musical understanding. One shortcut is to learn licks: short phrases borrowed from other musicians that you memorize and use over similar chord changes.

I really like this painting by Mondrian.

He had spent years developing a very strict visual language: straight lines, right angles, and a limited set of colors. Then he moved to New York, became interested in boogie-woogie music, and made this painting.

The grid is still there, but somehow it feels like it's dancing.

What I find interesting is that the structure itself didn't disappear. He was still working with constraints. But those constraints didn't prevent him from creating something new.

Jazz is similar. We have chord progressions, scales, and rhythmic structures. We all work within them, but the music can still sound completely different depending on who's playing.

I think science is similar too. We work with established theories, mathematical tools, and existing methods. Using them doesn't make our work less creative.

The interesting question is what we actually do with those tools.

## Two ways of learning a jazz solo

When jazz musicians practice improvisation, one common approach is to transcribe and copy solos by great players, like Charlie Parker.

I think this is a great way to learn. And if I had more time, I'd love to spend much more of it doing this myself.

But there are different ways to approach it.

Imagine someone who memorizes hundreds of phrases from Parker's solos. They know exactly which phrase works over which chord progression. When they improvise, they can quickly pull out the right phrase at the right moment.

And they might sound fantastic.

Now imagine someone else who spends more time asking questions. Why did Parker choose this particular note? Why did he resolve the phrase here instead of there? Why did he shift the rhythm in that way? What happens if we change the underlying harmony?

Instead of just collecting phrases, this person tries to understand the ideas behind them and use those ideas to create something of their own.

Which one is the better jazz player?

Well, I don't know. The first person might actually give a much better performance.

And if someone asks me whether the first person is a great performer, I would probably say yes.

But if someone asks whether that person is a great artist, I might think a little longer before answering.

I'm not saying memorizing phrases is wrong, or that using existing musical ideas makes someone less of an artist. In fact, you can learn a lot of musical structure just by copying and playing. And two musicians can play exactly the same notes but make completely different music through their timing, articulation, and interaction with the band.

Still, I think there is a meaningful distinction here.

Being able to produce something good and understanding why it works are not necessarily the same ability.

And perhaps more importantly, understanding something and making your own creative decisions with it are not quite the same thing either.

## What if we do the same thing in science?

Now let's imagine doing research with AI.

Suppose I give an AI a research problem. It comes up with hypotheses, designs the experiments, writes the code, analyzes the results, and eventually produces a really good paper.

The results are correct, the experiments are convincing, and the paper makes a genuine contribution to science.

That's great. I don't see any reason to deny the scientific value of the work just because AI produced it.

But what about me?

If my contribution was simply reading the final paper and saying, "Looks good, let's submit," would I feel like I had actually done science?

Probably not.

I might have produced a good paper. But I would have a hard time feeling that I was the scientist behind it.

On the other hand, suppose I use AI for almost all the coding, simulations, literature reviews, and even some of the theoretical derivations. But I am still actively thinking about the problem, questioning the assumptions, checking whether the conclusions make sense, and deciding what to investigate next.

That feels very different to me.

I don't think being a scientist requires doing everything by hand. We've always relied on tools, collaborators, and accumulated knowledge.

What matters to me is whether I'm actually participating in the scientific reasoning and decision-making.

## But understanding doesn't guarantee better performance

There's another part of this that I find important.

We often assume that someone who understands the underlying principles will perform better. But that isn't always true.

A jazz musician with an enormous vocabulary of memorized phrases might improvise better than someone who has spent years studying music theory.

Similarly, an AI might produce better scientific results than a human who has a much deeper understanding of a particular subject.

And remember our assumption: we're imagining an AI that does understand the physics. Perhaps it even understands the physics better than we do.

So I don't think understanding can be our final defense against AI.

We cannot just say, "Well, humans understand things, while AI only recognizes patterns." Even if that were a useful distinction for some systems today, there's no guarantee it will remain true.

Instead, I think we should separate two questions:

1. Can you produce a good result?
2. Can you explain, question, and make decisions about the principles behind that result?

These are different abilities. And I don't think one always guarantees the other.

But even if AI eventually becomes excellent at both, the distinction is still useful when thinking about our own involvement in research.

## Scientific agency

I've recently found myself thinking less about what makes someone a "real scientist" and more about something I'd call **scientific agency**.

For me, science isn't just about generating correct answers.

It's also about deciding which questions are worth asking. Being suspicious of an assumption that everyone else takes for granted. Recognizing that a beautiful theory might be wrong. Sometimes even deciding that the problem we've been trying to solve isn't the right problem in the first place.

In jazz, playing a beautiful solo over a given chord progression is one thing. Reinterpreting the harmony, responding to what another musician just played, or changing the direction of the music is another.

Of course, those things are not completely separate. Even a small change in timing can be a creative decision.

I think science works in a similar way.

And importantly, I'm not claiming that AI will never be able to do these things.

Under our assumption, AI might be able to make all these decisions, perhaps better than I can.

But that doesn't mean I have to stop making them myself.

I don't necessarily need to be the smartest or most efficient problem solver in the room to participate meaningfully in science.

This is where my thinking has started to shift. Instead of asking, "What can I do that AI cannot?", I want to ask, "What do I actually want to think about, understand, and create?"

That seems like a much more meaningful question to me.

## A couple of questions I still don't know how to answer

There are at least two questions I'm still struggling with.

First, should we evaluate scientists based on the knowledge they produce, or based on their ability to discover and understand knowledge?

Historically, these two were closely related. Producing a good scientific paper was usually pretty strong evidence that someone had engaged in meaningful scientific thinking.

AI is making that connection much weaker.

Perhaps we need to become more careful about distinguishing the value of a scientific result from the contribution of the person who presents it.

Second, suppose AI discovers something completely new and then explains everything to me. I follow the explanation, understand the theory, and can even reproduce the results.

Have I done science? Or have I simply learned something that someone, or something, else discovered?

I'm not sure where the boundary is.

Learning is obviously an important part of becoming a scientist. And understanding someone else's discovery can lead to entirely new questions.

But I think there is still a difference between understanding an answer and participating in the process of discovering it.

Maybe that difference matters more to me personally than it does for evaluating scientific progress as a whole.

## What kind of researcher do I want to be?

I remember reading a LinkedIn post a while ago. Unfortunately, I don't remember who wrote it, but the idea stayed with me.

It was something along the lines of: we don't remember Michelangelo by counting how many sculptures he made. We remember *David*.

Of course, that's a simplified way of talking about an artist's career. But I like the message.

I don't want to approach research as a game of producing as many papers as possible.

I want to make something I can be genuinely proud of.

Something I spent time thinking about. Something that changed how I understand a problem. Something I can explain, defend, and hopefully build upon.

And I don't mean that research becomes more valuable just because I spent more hours on it. I would gladly use AI to save time on things that don't require my attention. In fact, I already do, and I expect to use it even more.

But I don't want to completely outsource the part I enjoy most: asking questions, being confused, trying to understand why something works, and occasionally realizing that I was asking the wrong question all along.

Maybe AI will eventually be better at all of those things too.

That's fine.

I still want to be part of the process.

Going back to jazz, I don't necessarily want to be the person who plays the most impressive solo every night.

I want to understand the music well enough to have something of my own to say.

And I think that's also how I want to approach science.
